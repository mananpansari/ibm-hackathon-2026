Government official impersonation: someone calls claiming to be from the police, MAS, ICA, MOH or a court. They say your bank account or identity is linked to a crime and you must move your money to a "safe" or "government" account or crypto wallet while they investigate. Real officers never ask you to transfer money, buy crypto, or keep the call secret.

Bank staff impersonation: a caller says they are from your bank's fraud team and that your account has been compromised. They ask you to move savings to a new account or wallet "for protection", or to read out a one-time password. Your bank will never ask you to move money to keep it safe.

Fake friend call: a message or call from an unknown number pretends to be a friend or relative who has changed phone numbers. After some chat they ask to borrow money urgently. Call your friend on their old number before you pay.

Investment scam: a stranger online, or a group chat, shows big returns from a crypto or trading platform. You are encouraged to deposit more and more, and withdrawals are blocked unless you pay "taxes" or "fees" first. Guaranteed high returns are a warning sign.

Romance scam: someone you met online builds a close relationship over weeks, then asks for money for an emergency, a flight, or a shared investment. You have never met them in person. Never send money to someone you have only met online.

Job scam: you are offered easy online work such as liking videos or reviewing products. After small early payouts you are asked to pay in money or crypto to "unlock" bigger tasks or your earnings. Real jobs do not ask you to pay to work.

E-commerce scam: a seller on a marketplace asks you to pay outside the platform, often by bank transfer or crypto, and the item never arrives. Pay through the platform's own checkout.

Tech support scam: a pop-up or caller says your computer or phone is infected and asks you to install remote-access software or pay for a fix in crypto or gift cards. Hang up and never give strangers control of your device.

Parcel and customs scam: a caller says a parcel in your name contains illegal items and transfers you to "police". You are told to pay a fine or move money to clear your name. This is a version of the government impersonation scam.

Loan scam: a lender offers a fast loan with no checks but asks for an upfront "processing fee" or "deposit" in crypto. Licensed moneylenders do not ask for money before giving a loan.

Phishing link: an SMS or email with a link asks you to log in to your bank or crypto account to fix a problem. The site steals your login. Type your bank's address yourself instead of tapping links.

Kidnap or emergency scam: a caller says a family member is hurt, arrested or kidnapped and demands immediate payment. Hang up and call the family member directly.

Recovery scam: after losing money to a scam, someone offers to recover it for a fee, often claiming to be a lawyer, a hacker or an agency. Paying them loses more money.

Wallet "verification" scam: you are told your crypto wallet must be verified, upgraded or synced, and asked to send coins to a given address or enter your recovery phrase. No real service needs your recovery phrase or a test payment.

Secrecy and pressure: in almost every scam you are told to act now, to keep it secret from family and from bank staff, and to stay on the phone while you pay. If someone tells you to lie to your bank, it is a scam.
